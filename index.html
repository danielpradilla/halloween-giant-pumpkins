What changed

How big can they get?

The sources below describe what changed between the 400 lb pumpkins of 1900 and today's. None of them predicts where the record will stop.

Selective breeding

Nova Scotia grower Howard Dill spent about 30 years selecting giant pumpkins from Mammoth varieties and developed Dill's Atlantic Giant. Oregon State University Extension: "Every world champion since has come from its offspring." Growers still name seeds after the pumpkin they came from: a "1050 Liggett" seed came from a 1,050 lb pumpkin.

Sources: Oregon State University Extension, Ohio State University fact sheet HYG-1646

Genetics

Giant pumpkins are Cucurbita maxima. A 2022 genomic study places the giant (mammoth) group as descended from the hubbard group, with genes from the buttercup group mixed in. A 2015 study found that giant varieties had no more leaf area or photosynthetic capacity than their ancestor. They had more phloem, the tissue that carries sugar, in their leaf and fruit stalks.

Sources: Reddy et al. 2022, Frontiers in Genetics, Savage, Haines & Holbrook 2015, Plant, Cell & Environment

Cultivation techniques

Ohio State's guide allows about 1,000 square feet (93 m²) per plant and, once the fruits reach volleyball size, cuts back to one pumpkin per plant. Growers pollinate by hand to control the cross, bury the vine's leaf nodes so they root, and shade the fruit to prevent scalding and splitting.

Sources: Ohio State University, Oregon State University Extension

Water and nutrients

According to Oregon State, one plant can cover 1,200 square feet and take up to 500 US gallons (about 1,900 litres) of water a week. Ohio State recommends about 2 lb of nitrogen, 3 lb of phosphate and 6 lb of potash per 1,000 square feet. At peak growth an Atlantic Giant can gain close to 50 lb (23 kg) a day, according to plant physiologist Jessica Savage.

Sources: Oregon State University Extension, Ohio State University, Smithsonian, 2015

Structural and biological limits

Giant pumpkins flatten as they grow. Hu et al. (2011) measured height-to-width ratios falling by up to 50% from small to large fruit, mostly through deformation at the base. Cracks tend to appear during the fastest phase of growth and after sudden changes in water content, such as rainstorms. The same paper's model of a perfectly uniform shell fails at about 3.3 m across and 9,000 kg, but the authors note that uneven walls may break much earlier, so that figure is not a forecast.

Source: Hu, Richards & Alexeev 2011, Int. J. Non-Linear Mechanics
